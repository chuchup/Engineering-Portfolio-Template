Project Description

In this project, I developed a prototype breathalyzer using an MQ-3 alcohol sensor, Arduino microcontroller, and LabVIEW interface. I designed the hardware to detect alcohol through the MQ-3 sensor, regulate the power supply, and use an LM741 comparator with an LED indicator to identify the presence of alcohol. I then programmed an Arduino Uno to perform analog-to-digital conversion and calculate the sensor resistance before communicating the measurements to LabVIEW. In LabVIEW, I designed a user interface to process the sensor output and estimate BAC from the measured BrAC. Although the complete data acquisition pipeline was not achieved, the project successfully demonstrated alcohol detection and the integration of hardware, microcontroller programming, and software visualization.

Skills Learned

This project gave me hands-on experience with sensor interfacing, analog circuit design, microcontroller programming, ADC, serial communication, and LabVIEW. I learned how to interpret a sensor datasheet and design supporting circuitry around the MQ-3, including voltage regulation and comparator-based signal detection. I also gained experience programming an Arduino to process analog sensor data and communicate with LabVIEW at 9600 baud. One of the most valuable aspects of the project was troubleshooting the integration between the hardware, microcontroller, and software. Issues with component compatibility, signal fluctuations, and the physical design of the device showed me the importance of considering the entire system—not just individual components—when developing an engineering prototype.

What's Next?

This project gave me the opportunity to build on the hardware experience I gained from the ECG lab and apply it to a more complete biomedical instrumentation system. I am especially proud of this project because we successfully integrated multiple components into one working prototype, including the MQ-3 alcohol sensor, analog hardware, an Arduino Uno, and a LabVIEW software interface. Compared to the ECG lab, I found the troubleshooting process much more manageable because I had developed a better understanding of how to approach physical circuits, test individual components, and identify where problems were occurring.

This project also showed me the importance of following a structured design process. Rather than trying to build everything at once, we worked through the hardware, microcontroller, and software components in stages and gradually integrated them together. My experience with the ECG taught me that problems are inevitable when working with real hardware, so I approached this project with more patience and a stronger troubleshooting mindset. Seeing the different parts of the system work together was especially rewarding and showed me how hardware and software can be combined to create a practical biomedical device. Overall, this project gave me greater confidence in my hardware skills and demonstrated how much I had improved through the challenges of the previous ECG project.
